Forget the pre-party panic and embrace the spooky; in this article on Halloween charcuterie board ideas, we’ll show you how to curate a haunted grazing table that’s visually striking, effortlessly assembled, and undeniably crave-worthy.

Think Zones, Not a Snack Formula

Skip the strict charcuterie rules. Build it like a haunted house guests wander through: a cauldron dip, a pumpkin patch, a crunchy graveyard, monster bites, a green swamp, a sweet corner, and maybe a witchy drink.

The goal is simple: give guests different flavors, textures, temperatures, and serving styles. Some people will want a hot bite. Some will want something creamy. Some will want fruit and crunch. Some will head straight for the cookie dough dip. That is the point.

Pick three or four zones for a smaller party, or go all in.

Start With One Big Visual Moment

Anchor your board with a single ‘showstopper’—a visual centerpiece that sets the eerie mood immediately. Instead of scattering themes randomly, let this one element dictate your color palette and vibe. 

For example, place a serving of Pumpkin Soup in pumpkin-shaped mugs as your pumpkin patch centerpiece, or set out a mini cauldron filled with Protein Pickle Dip or Cottage Cheese Queso. Alternatively, build a sweet crypt anchored by Cottage Cheese Cookie Dough, or feature a dramatic pitcher of Ginger Citrus Mocktail as your witches’ brew. 

Once your anchor is set, it serves as the gravitational pull for the rest of your snacks, making it easy to arrange extras like tombstone bites or eyeball eggs around it.

Choose Two or Three Recipes, Max

You do not need seven different homemade dishes to impress; two or three are plenty to anchor a balanced spread. To build a cohesive board with zero overwhelm, pick one item from each of these three categories:

  • The Savory Anchor: Start with a hearty, dippable base like the Protein Pickle Dip or Cottage Cheese Queso.
  • The Substantial Bite: Choose one warm, protein-packed main, such as Air-Fryer Boneless Buffalo Wings or Chickpea Salad Sandwich bites for a plant-forward option.
  • The Sweet Finish: Balance the salt with something creamy like Cottage Cheese Cookie Dough or a refreshing Ginger Citrus Mocktail.

“A grazing table should still feel like enough food, not just a collection of bites. Adding a few protein-rich options, like deviled eggs, wings, yogurt-based dips, cheese, or chickpea bites, can help the spread feel more satisfying,” Emily Sullivan, RD, MyFitnessPal Registered Dietitian, says. (3) 

By sticking to this ‘one savory, one hearty, one sweet’ formula, you ensure a variety of textures and flavors while keeping your prep time manageable. Need more inspiration? You can browse endless options via MyFitnessPal Recipe Discovery.

Make It Feel Grown-Up

Halloween food gets cartoonish fast. Ground it with marinated olives, sharp cheese, spicy pickles, herbed nuts, whole-grain crackers, roasted red peppers, a fresh-herb dip, or a mocktail in a coupe glass.

Then add just one or two playful touches: olive spiders, candy eyes, or skeleton hands by the tongs.

Keep the food recognizable. Just give it a costume.

How To Arrange It

Start with anything in cups or bowls. That means the pumpkin soup cups, dips, olives, pickles, nuts, and sweets. These pieces give the board height and shape.

If you are serving soup in pumpkin-shaped mugs, set them on a tray or platter first so they are easier to move and refill.

Then place the warm or prepped foods, each in its own spot. Fill the gaps with crackers, apple slices, grapes, carrots, cucumbers, celery, pepper strips, berries, and mini chocolate.

Add the Halloween details last because cracker tombstones can go soggy fast. Keep candy in one corner so it looks intentional.

Keep It Safe

Refresh the board instead of letting it sit untouched for hours.

You do not need every item on the board to be themed. One strong visual moment, like pumpkin soup cups or a cauldron dip, can make the whole table feel festive.

“Cold foods should stay at 40°F or colder, hot foods should stay at 140°F or warmer, and perishable foods should be tossed if they have been out for more than 2 hours unless they are kept hot or cold,” Sullivan says (2).

That covers dips, cheese, wings, and anything egg- or dairy-based.

Easy fix: put out smaller portions, keep backups in the fridge, and refresh the board halfway through the party.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

  • What are good Halloween appetizers for adults?

Savory dips, mini sandwiches, wings, skewers, stuffed peppers, cheese, olives, spiced nuts, vegetables, crackers, and a sweet or two all work well. Aim for foods that are easy to eat one-handed while mingling. For more variety, include a mix of protein foods, dairy, vegetables, fruit, healthy fats such as nuts, seeds and olives,, and whole grains. (1)

  • How do I make a board look spooky?

Use dark trays, black bowls, cracker tombstones, olive spiders, candy eyes, skeleton hands, or a spooky label. You do not need every food to be themed. A few details can carry the whole board.

  • How far ahead can I prep?

Wash produce, make dips, mix the mocktail base, and cut bread or crackers ahead. Add sparkling water last, and wait to assemble anything crunchy until closer to serving.

  • How long can it sit out?

Perishable foods can sit out for up to 2 hours unless they are kept hot or cold. If the temperature is above 90°F, that window drops to 1 hour. (2)

Bottom Line

Skip the standard snack-board formula.

Build in zones: a pumpkin patch, cauldron dip, swamp crunch, monster bites, tombstone sandwiches, a sweet crypt, and a witches’ brew. Anchor it with a few MyFitnessPal recipes, fill the rest with easy staples, and add just enough spooky styling to make it feel festive.

The best board is the one people actually gather around.

Let’s face it: McDonald’s is everywhere. Whether you’re making a quick Micky D’s run to fuel up for a busy day or your body is craving the McNuggets you fell in love with as a kid, it’s normal to dine now and then at one of the most recognizable fast food chains on the planet.

The good news is that you’ll find some options at McDonald’s that will let you enjoy your favorite fast food restaurant without derailing your nutrition and weight loss progress. We’ve rounded up the 9 healthy McDonald’s food orders from breakfast through dinner so you always know what to reach for.

Is There Healthy Food at McDonald’s?

Yes, you can find healthier options at McDonald’s. It’s possible by choosing lower-calorie items and smaller portions. (1)

Fast food is often high in sodium, calories, and fat. Too many of these things can affect both your mental and physical health. That’s why moderation is key. If you crave those iconic fries or a classic burger, it’s okay to indulge once in a while. Making smart choices and simple swaps at fast food joints like McDonald’s means you can honor your cravings in a way that feels good for your mind and body. (2, 3, 4)

Here are some handy things to remember the next time you order at McDonald’s:

  • Whether you choose chicken or beef, the protein content will be similar but from a fat and calorie standpoint, the chicken sandwiches aren’t always the lighter choice, so make sure to check the nutrition info. (1)
  • It can be tempting to supersize your meal, but sticking to smaller portions keeps your wellness journey order on track and will help you feel good about your choices later.
  • When scanning for healthier McDonald’s options, aim for items under 400 calories; you’ll find more than a few on the menu below. (1)

The Healthiest Food at McDonald’s: 9 Dietitian-Approved Picks

Katherine Basbaum is a registered dietitian who works with MyFitnessPal. We asked her what she looks for when considering the healthiest McDonald’s items.

“The healthiest options, in my view, tend to include the menu items that aren’t loaded with a ton of sauces and cheese as well as those with options for reduced serving sizes (4-piece instead of 10) and a lower calorie count (400ish and below),” Basbaum explains. (20)

Keeping that in mind, some of these picks are the healthiest food at McDonald’s that follow Basbaum’s guidelines. We’ve covered you for breakfast, lunch, and dinner so that you can enjoy McDonald’s anytime!

1. Fruit and Maple Oatmeal

Snag healthy McDonald’s food for breakfast with a bowl of fruit and maple oatmeal. Oats have tons of health benefits since they’re high in antioxidants, are a source of whole-grain fiber, and can help lower cholesterol. 

“When I think of healthy food at McDonald’s, their Fruit & Maple Oatmeal is at the top of my list. The main ingredients are fresh, whole foods that come together to create a warm, filling breakfast under 400 calories. The rolled oats provide fiber and complex carbohydrates, natural sweetness comes from the fresh and dried fruit, and there’s just a touch of light cream and brown sugar for a bit of richness,” Basbaum says. 

For these reasons, oatmeal is considered one of the healthiest breakfast items to help you start your day. (5) This oatmeal has 320 calories, 4.5 grams of fat, and 6 grams of protein, making it an all-around healthy choice. (6)

2. Egg McMuffin

If you like a savory fast-food breakfast, the original Egg McMuffin is one of the top McDonald’s healthier options to consider. It has 310 calories, 13 grams of fat, and 17 grams of protein. 

This could be a better option if you’re counting macros, as it has 30 grams of carbs compared to oatmeal, which has 64. (6,7)

Thanks to the egg, cheese, and lean Canadian bacon, it also has a nice protein boost. In fact, at 17 grams of protein for just 310 calories, the Egg McMuffin is one of the highest-protein breakfast options at McDonald’s.(1,7)

3. 4-piece Chicken McNuggets With Small Fries

McDonald’s chicken nuggets are iconic. If McNuggets are your jam, go for the 4-piece size, which has just 170 calories, 10 grams of fat, and 9 grams of protein, making it one of the lowest-calorie healthier options at McDonald’s on the menu. “Chicken options like the McNuggets,” notes Basbaum, “generally contain much less saturated fat than the options that go heavy on the beef and cheese.” (1,8)

And thanks to the low-calorie count, you can pair your nuggets with a side of small fries and still keep your meal under 500 calories. An order of small fries has 230 calories and 11 grams of fat, not including dipping sauces like ketchup. (8,9)

4. Filet-O-Fish Sandwich

The Filet-O-Fish is a McDonald’s classic with 380 calories, 19 grams of fat, and 16 grams of protein. (10)

Even though it’s better to eat broiled or seared to save on fat, fish has several health benefits and is usually quite lean. You can ask for it without cheese or sauce to lower the fat content or enjoy it as is. (11,12)

5. Classic Hamburger: The Healthiest Burger at McDonald’s

When thinking about the lowest-calorie food at McDonald’s, you might be surprised to discover that the classic burger has just 250 calories. With the included toppings, it has nine grams of fat and 12 grams of protein, which isn’t too bad. It’s widely considered the healthiest burger at McDonald’s (1,13,15)

As with the nuggets, you can pair a burger with small fries and still come in under 500 calories. (9,13)

6. Apple Slices

A side of apple slices is the healthiest food at McDonald’s, so feel free to pair it with anything else on the menu. One serving has just 15 calories, zero fat, and just 4 grams of carbs. When considering sides, it’s a much healthier choice than fries or other sweet treats. (1,9,14)

7. Hash browns

Perfectly golden and crispy, hash browns are hard to resist when stopping by McDonald’s in the morning for breakfast. Each has 140 calories, 8 grams of fat, and 2 grams of protein. A hash brown alone might not fill you up, so pair it with any of the other breakfast items mentioned on this list and still enjoy an entire meal for under 500 calories. (16)

8. Sausage Burrito

If you’re craving something a little different for breakfast, try the sausage burrito, which has 310 calories, 17 grams of fat, and 13 grams of protein. It’s a nice alternative to a breakfast sandwich filled with eggs and sausage for protein and peppers and onions for a bit of veggies. You’ll also find some melty cheese inside, which always makes everything taste better! (17)

9. 3-Piece McCrispy Strips

If you’re looking for a satisfying lunch or dinner option with a good amount of protein, McCrispy Strips deserve a spot on your radar. With 350 calories, 17 grams of fat, and a whopping 30 grams of protein, a 3-piece order is one of the best high-protein healthier options at McDonald’s under 400 calories. (1,18)

McDonald’s describes McCrispy Strips as crispy, juicy, peppery chicken strips made with all white meat chicken and coated with golden-brown breading and black pepper flavor. (18)

“Chicken options generally contain a lower ratio of saturated fat than the options that go heavy on the beef and cheese,” Basbaum reminds us and the 3-piece McCrispy Strips are a prime example. Just be mindful of dipping sauces, which can add extra calories depending on what you choose. (1)

Make Healthy Eating at McDonald’s a Habit

The bottom line is that you can fit in the occasional stop at  McDonald’s when you’re trying to make healthier choices. As Basbaum reminds us, the trick is to stick with McDonald’s higher-protein lower-calorie items and practice mindfulness and moderation to enjoy what you’re eating in the moment and not overthink things. 

“But for the ultimate in wholesome ingredients, balanced nutrition and mindful preparation,” Basbaum says, “there’s simply no place like home.” (8,13,14,18)

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

Can you eat healthy food at McDonald’s?

You can make healthier choices at McDonald’s that fit into a balanced diet, depending on your nutritional needs and preferences. The classic hamburger and 4-piece Chicken McNuggets are two options that can be paired with a side to create a meal. Rather than focusing on calories alone, consider the overall nutritional value of your meal, including protein, fiber, sodium, and other nutrients, as well as portion sizes. (1,8,13)

What is the lowest calorie thing at McDonald’s?

Apple slices are the single lowest-calorie item at just 15 calories, but if you’re looking for a complete meal, the classic hamburger (250 calories, 12g protein) or the 3-piece McCrispy Strips (350 cal, 30g protein) are among the most balanced options on the menu. Pair either with apple slices instead of fries to keep the meal well under 500 calories. (1,13,14,18)

Does McDonald’s have healthy breakfast choices?

Though perhaps not as healthy as the light and nutritious breakfast you’d make at home, McDonald’s breakfast includes some genuinely balanced choices. The Egg McMuffin (310 calories, 17g protein) and the Fruit and Maple Oatmeal (320 calories, 6g protein, whole-grain fiber) stand out as the best picks. The key is skipping the oversized combo meals and high-sodium sausage platters and sticking to single items under 400 calories. (1,6,7)

What is the healthiest burger at McDonald’s?

The classic hamburger is the healthiest burger at McDonald’s, with just 250 calories, 9g of fat, and 12g of protein, which is the lowest calorie count of any burger on the menu. If you want a bit more protein, the McDouble adds a second patty for around 400 calories and 22g of protein, still a reasonable choice by fast food standards. (1,13,15,19)

Can a one-bowl meal really satisfy? This one will. It starts with a base of nutty farro and tender sweet potato, topped with chicken breast and finished with a tangy, herbal chimichurri sauce.

Farro is a great grain option because it contains a good amount of protein and fiber (1). You can also add any roasted vegetable — like broccoli or squash — to this meal to up the fiber content.

Active time: 45 minutes | Total time: 1 hour

Chicken and Sweet Potato Farro Bowl

Ingredients

  • 1 cup (208g) semi-pearled farro
  • 1 medium (130g) sweet potato, cubed (and peeled if desired)
  • 1 lb (450g) boneless, skinless chicken breast
  • 1 tbsp olive oil, divided
  • 2 tsp chili powder
  • 1 1/2 cups (36g) fresh parsley, lightly packed
  • 1 clove garlic, chopped
  • 1/4 tsp crushed red pepper flakes
  • 3 tbsp red wine vinegar

Directions

Preheat the oven to 400ºF (200ºC).

Put 3 cups of water in a medium saucepan and bring to a boil. Add the farro and sweet potato cubes and return to a boil, then cover and reduce the heat to low to simmer for 20 minutes. The farro should be tender. Drain in a wire mesh strainer and let cool. The mixture can be stored in the refrigerator, tightly covered, for up to a week.

Line a sheet pan with parchment, then place the chicken on the pan. Drizzle with 1 tsp olive oil and massage to coat, then sprinkle with the chili powder and rub to coat. Bake for 15–20 minutes, until the chicken is cooked through and an instant-read thermometer reads 165°F (74°C) when inserted in the thickest part of the breast. Cool on a rack until safe to handle. Shred into pieces and reserve.

For the chimichurri, place the parsley and garlic in the bowl of a food processor. Process to mince finely, scraping down the sides to make a fine paste. Add the crushed red pepper, red wine vinegar and remaining 2 tsp of olive oil. Process until smooth. (Makes about 1/2 cup.) 

Serves: 4 | Serving Size: 1 cup farro and sweet potato mixture, plus 1 cup chicken and 2 tbsp chimichurri sauce

Colostrum vs. Collagen: Which One Should You Take?

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A surprisingly common query I get is colostrum vs. collagen—which is better? Maybe it’s the fact that they both start with the same three letters. Maybe it’s that they’re both alternative health darlings currently enjoying a huge uptick in interest.

Subscribe now

Whatever the reason for their connection in people’s minds, I thought it would be a good idea to do a quick write-up on how colostrum and collagen might interact in the body, what the relative benefits are, and whether you can take them both together.

Let’s dive right in.

Where Do Colostrum and Collagen Benefits Overlap?

Contrary to what the influencers might tell you, there isn’t a ton of research showing that bovine colostrum has a wide range of benefits for adults. It’s definitely helpful for newborns, since their guts are incredibly permeable, and colostrum is literally the first food they’re supposed to eat. Even cow colostrum has been shown to improve outcomes for newborns and premature infants. No arguments there.

But I’d caution against expecting too much from colostrum supplements. The best case for adding it to your diet as an adult is improving gut health, reducing intestinal permeability, and improving immune function—particularly for athletes.

Colostrum has been heavily researched for its ability to reduce gut permeability in various populations, including athletes, medical patients, and everyday folks.1-4 People who take bovine colostrum supplements often report better gut health and a more intact gut lining. Zonulin, a gut compound that increases permeability, frequently drops with colostrum intake.

Researchers aren’t entirely sure how bovine colostrum benefits the gut. Some people claim it regulates specific growth factors in the gut lining that help repair and support its structure. That’s colostrum’s primary role in infancy: improving gut viability so the immune system can develop, enabling digestion of food instead of relying on the womb for sustenance. Thanks in large part to colostrum benefits, this new member of the human race is able to handle what the world throws at it and develop a healthy immune response and gut lining

The challenge is that the adult gut isn’t as permeable as a newborn’s by design. For instance, the IGF-1 in bovine colostrum isn’t absorbed or utilized by adults.5

Colostrum can also regulate inflammation and help maintain iron balance in athletes, largely due to its lactoferrin content.6 Lactoferrin is an immune protein that improves iron absorption while reducing free iron—an inflammatory agent—circulating in the body. Athletes taking colostrum for instance see higher spikes in inflammation during workouts but lower resting inflammation, showing it promotes “smart” inflammation.

Meanwhile, collagen benefits for gut health are much more direct. Collagen provides the actual building blocks—amino acids like glycine—for repairing and growing the gut lining. Whether it turns out that colostrum does indeed trigger growth factors that help the gut repair itself (by no means confirmed), or if it just lowers inflammation via lactoferrin which allows the repair to proceed, you’ll need extra collagen to make those repairs.

Colostrum assists with gut repair while collagen provides a substrate required for the repair to occur.

And so the two compounds are not competing with each other, but potentially complementary. Both colostrum and collagen improve gut health, enhance skin health and appearance, and boost immune function by different but complementary routes.

Can You Take Them Together?

Yes, you can absolutely take collagen and colostrum together. In fact, I’d argue that if colostrum works, they’re almost made for each other. You don’t need to mix them in the same shake or take them simultaneously; just include both in your routine for maximum benefit.

Adding both colostrum and collagen supplements can create a synergistic effect, particularly for gut and immune health. Collagen serves as the substrate, while colostrum provides potential activation via growth factors.

When Is One More Useful Than the Other?

It depends on your dietary needs. If you only take one, take collagen. Collagen is like the fourth macronutrient, providing glycine—an amino acid we no longer get enough of in modern diets. This makes collagen essential for maintaining skin, bones, blood vessels, and more. It’s also widely beneficial, whether you’re looking for joint health, anti-aging, or gut support.

If you regularly consume foods like bone broth, gelatinous meats, or stews, you may already be getting plenty of collagen. In that case, colostrum supplements could add more value, especially for individuals with immune or gut issues that dietary changes haven’t resolved.

Colostrum, derived from the mature milk of cows after giving birth, is essentially absent from the diet unless you specifically seek it out. It’s best suited for people dealing with gut inflammation or immune dysregulation. Athletes might benefit from colostrum’s ability to regulate iron and inflammation, while others may see improved resistance to respiratory infections.

Here’s How I Break It Down:

Who should take collagen?

Everyone.

Who should take colostrum?

  • Athletes with iron balance issues.

  • Adults prone to upper respiratory tract infections.

  • Individuals with leaky gut or gut inflammation.

If you’re on a budget, you can likely replicate most of the colostrum supplement benefits with a high-quality whey isolate (which contains some lactoferrin, but not as much as colostrum) and a dedicated lactoferrin supplement. My guess is that lactoferrin is doing much of the heavy lifting for colostrum’s benefits.

Final Thoughts on Colostrum vs. Collagen

Both colostrum and collagen offer unique benefits, but collagen stands out as the more universally essential supplement. Its role in collagen production, gut lining repair, and overall health makes it indispensable for most people. Bovine colostrum can complement collagen, especially in specific scenarios, but it isn’t as necessary or widely applicable.

For most, collagen is the clear winner. Still, if you have unique needs or simply want to experiment with bovine colostrum benefits, adding both to your regimen can offer the best of both worlds.

A New Strike Against High Protein?

📅 Sep 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A recent paper is fueling the plant-based crowd. In it, the authors found that protein restriction turned off many genetic and molecular signals of aging.

It was a synthesis of over 350 existing studies, a mix of in vitro research, animal research, and human trials, and the overall finding was that restricting protein elicited favorable levels and changes to mTOR, FGF21, oxidative stress, and other things associated with or contributory to aging.

Subscribe now

Now, the study had some interesting things, right? There was some nuance there. They readily admitted that protein restriction acts differently in sedentary versus active individuals. Those who are actively training have a higher supposedly “safe” window of protein consumption. Maybe they have a higher tolerance for protein. But overall, the thrust of the paper, and what most people will take away from it, and what your vegan friends are no doubt bragging to you about, is that less protein is better for longevity.

I don’t think it’s as simple as that. Let me explain.

Does Eating Meat Reduce Longevity?

Much of the research rests on the assumption that specific amino acids in excess promote aging. The one they say most is methionine. It’s found in dairy, muscle meat, eggs, and other animal products, and absolutely, excessive amounts of it can promote aging in animal models, and likely humans. No argument there.

But what these studies never explore is the reality that methionine is balanced by another amino acid: glycine. Glycine is almost entirely absent from the classic animal protein products like meat or eggs or dairy, and it’s found almost exclusively in connective tissue, bones, and skin.

There is a classic study where they put rodents on a high-methionine diet, and sure enough, those rats died pretty young. But they took another group and gave them glycine, same amount of methionine, just with extra glycine, and those guys lived long, healthy lives, just as long as the rodents who lived on a restricted-methionine diet.1-2

We see echoes of this in the human literature. Tons of research finds that low glycine levels in the serum predict type 2 diabetes, heart disease, and many of the other diseases classically linked to meat consumption.

Now let’s look at Hong Kong, which is one of the longest-living populations on Earth, who also happen to eat the most meat of anyone on Earth. But the meat they’re eating is not just steaks and burgers and roasts. They’re also eating skin, snouts, feet, and other animal foods that are high in glycine.

This is why collagen is so essential for the modern meat eater who doesn’t want to eat the obscure stuff like skin and bones. Thirty to 40 grams of collagen a day provides enough glycine to balance out all the muscle meat and other animal protein you eat. And although we don’t have direct human trials of balancing out methionine with glycine and looking at lifespan, the circumstantial evidence is pretty strong that it’s going to have good effects.

Share Mark's Daily Apple

Except that in the real world, when you see people who actually practice this, these are people who live this lifestyle, they’ve read the studies, they’ve convinced themselves that protein restriction is key to living long. They are generally pretty unwell. They’re frail, always cold, have low energy, and generally aren’t the picture of health we are imagining for ourselves.

Now, they may be better off and look better than the general population in some ways. They tend to be thinner, at least, and, you know, even their biomarkers look pretty good. But I refuse to believe that this is the path to optimal health. You may limp by a bit longer. You may slowly walk into old age, but can you still run hills? Can you play? Are you strong?

And that’s where the study loses me.

My Beef with the Findings

Nothing against the authors themselves. It’s pretty impossible to cover every angle, and as I said before, they did a pretty fair job of noting that active exercisers have a higher tolerance of protein. But the true path to health is on the teeter-totter, the seesaw, the waxing and the waning.

It’s a big steak after a workout and then maybe skipping breakfast the next day and just having some fish with salad for lunch. It’s periods of abundance and periods of restriction. It is high-protein days and lower-protein days. We see this everywhere. We see it in wild animals, predators who eat and gorge themselves for a day or two and then go for several days without eating anything. You see this in the fasting habits of monks and other religious men. Christianity even has protein fasting built into it.

No, I’m not saying that you should fast for three days after you eat a big steak like a tiger or wolf, or eliminate protein for a 14-day period. I’m just saying that the world is cyclical. That seems embedded in our environment and also our biology.

This is why, over the years, I have settled on not daily protein targets, but four- to five-day protein targets, even weekly protein targets. I want to eat an average of 120 grams of protein a day over the course of a week. Some days it’ll be 180, other days it’ll be 70. It doesn’t matter so much day-to-day as it does across the week.

Do studies like this give you pause, or do you remain confident—as I do—in the healthfulness of an “high” (read, “sufficient”) protein omnivorous diet? Let me know in the comments.

Definitive Guide: The Primal Blueprint

📅 Apr 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
Build the Healthiest Possible Body with the Primal Blueprint

I get emails every day from people who are changing their lives for the better by following the guidelines I outline on Mark’s Daily Apple. Many are looking for more of what the Primal Blueprint has to offer. That is to say, they want a comprehensive breakdown of the elements that make up the Blueprint—a Primal primer, if you will.

In this extended article, you will find the basic building blocks needed to discover the Primal side of your life.

What does this mean?

  • It means learning and understanding what it means to be human.

  • It means using this knowledge to help you make important lifestyle choices.

  • It means modeling your life after your ancestors in order to promote optimal health and wellness.

  • And, most importantly, it means taking control of your body and mind.

It is my hope that you will use this information as a jumping-off point to build the healthiest possible body with the Primal Blueprint. Then share it with friends and loved ones who have yet to discover how they can use the Primal Blueprint to live awesome.

What Is the Primal Blueprint?

My basic premise is this:

The Primal Blueprint is a set of simple instructions that allows you to control how your genes express themselves in order to build the strongest, healthiest body possible, taking clues from evolutionary biology.

Sometimes we get so lost in the science of human biology that we just can’t see the forest for the trees. We overlook the simplicity and ease with which we could all be achieving exceptional health and fitness.

Living in modern society is extremely complex. With daily mind-boggling achievements in science, technology, and medicine, it’s no wonder that we often look for complicated scientific solutions to problems that really only require simple answers.

Of course, we should all be profoundly grateful for the advances that allow us to recover and heal from treatable infectious disease and trauma. Yet the fact remains that most of life is really much simpler than modern medicine and science would like to have you believe.

You can have a tremendous impact on how your genes express themselves simply by providing your cells with the right environments.

All you need is:

  • A basic understanding of how your body works

  • A simple philosophical roadmap to guide your health and fitness decisions

Primal Blueprint Origins and the Rules of Living Today

You may already have a fair understanding of how the human genome evolved to where it is today based on the environmental and behavioral factors that shaped our ancestors’ lives.

Anthropologists, evolutionary biologists, paleontologists, geneticists, and others have worked for over 100 years to piece together a detailed picture of the elements that influenced our development as a species.

Ironically, when we examine all these influences, we arrive at a very simple list of general behaviors our early ancestors followed.

This list is essentially the original Primal Blueprint—the behaviors that enabled them to become healthy, robust, and resilient human beings, and to pass those traits down to us.

The 10 Primal Blueprint Laws

The goal of the modern Primal Blueprint is not to revert to a caveman lifestyle.

Instead, it asks:

How can we learn from our past to shape a healthier future?

These 10 laws form the foundation of optimal health and longevity.

1. Eat lots of animals, insects, and plants

Focus on:

  • Quality protein (meat, poultry, fish)

  • Colorful vegetables

  • Some fruits (mostly berries)

  • Healthy fats (avocado, olive oil, nuts, full-fat dairy if tolerated)

Observe portion control over time rather than meal by meal.

2. Avoid poisonous things

Limit exposure to:

  • Chemical toxins (pesticides, herbicides, fungicides)

  • Processed foods

  • Sugar and grains

  • Trans and hydrogenated fats

  • Mercury in certain fish

3. Move around a lot (mostly at a slow pace)

Walk as much as possible every day.

Aim for 2 to 5 hours per week of low-level aerobic activity:

  • Walking

  • Hiking

  • Easy cycling

  • Swimming

4. Lift heavy things

Spend 30–45 minutes, 2–3 times per week lifting weights or resistance.

Focus on full-body movements:

  • Pushing

  • Pulling

  • Carrying

  • Climbing

5. Run really fast every once in a while

Do occasional high-intensity sprint bursts, such as:

  • Short hill sprints

  • Beach sprints

  • Intense cycling intervals

6. Get lots of sleep

Sleep is critical for:

  • Energy

  • Immune function

  • Overall health

Prioritize it.

7. Play

Engage in active play regularly.

Play helps:

  • Reduce stress

  • Improve creativity

  • Apply fitness in real life

8. Get some sunlight every day

Moderate sun exposure helps:

  • Produce vitamin D

  • Improve mood

Avoid burning, but don’t avoid the sun completely.

9. Avoid stupid mistakes

Eliminate obvious risks:

  • Wear seat belts

  • Avoid drugs and smoking

  • Stay aware of your surroundings

10. Use your mind

Keep your brain active:

  • Read

  • Write

  • Create

  • Socialize

Why Follow the Primal Blueprint?

Let’s simplify the goal.

We all want to be:

  • Healthy — free from illness

  • Energetic — able to enjoy life fully

  • Happy — motivated and positive

  • Lean — metabolically balanced

  • Strong — physically capable

  • Bright — mentally sharp

  • Productive — contributing to life and society

Our ancestors naturally embodied these traits—not as goals, but as a result of how they lived.

Now, understanding that everything we do affects our biology, we can apply these principles to modern life and create the same outcomes.
