Chilled buckwheat soba noodles are traditionally served as a cooling meal in the heat of summer in Japan. In this salad, the noodles are tossed with plenty of veggies, grilled chicken and a gingery dressing. 

RD Tip: Be sure to buy noodles with a high percentage of whole-grain buckwheat flour for the most beneficial fiber.

Active time: 35 minutes | Total time: 45 minutes

Soba Noodle Salad With Ginger Dressing

Ingredients

  • 2 tbsp low-sodium soy sauce
  • 2 tbsp rice vinegar
  • 1 tbsp honey
  • 2 tsp toasted sesame oil
  • 3 tsp ginger, finely grated
  • 3 medium garlic cloves, finely chopped
  • 1 tbsp canola oil
  • 3 cups (85g) shiitake mushrooms, stems discarded, caps sliced
  • 1/4 tsp salt
  • 1/4 tsp ground black pepper
  • 2 (8 oz/227g each) boneless, skinless chicken breasts
  • 4 cups (227g) broccoli crowns, cut into 2-inch florets
  • 1/2 cup (75g) shelled edamame
  • 6 oz (170g) dry buckwheat soba noodles 
  • 3 green onions, thinly sliced

Directions

In a small bowl, combine the soy sauce, vinegar, honey, sesame oil, ginger and garlic. Set aside.

Heat the canola oil in a cast-iron skillet or heavy sauté pan over medium-high heat. Add the mushrooms and sauté until tender, 4 minutes. Transfer to a large serving bowl. Season the chicken with the salt and pepper before adding it to the skillet, reduce the heat to medium, and cook until cooked through and an instant-read thermometer registers 165°F (74°C), about 5 minutes per side. Place on a cutting board and let rest, uncovered, for 10 minutes.

While the chicken rests, bring a medium saucepan of water to a boil. Add the broccoli and edamame and cook until the broccoli is crisp-tender, 4 minutes. With a slotted spoon, transfer the vegetables to the serving bowl with the mushrooms. Add the soba noodles to the boiling water and cook according to package instructions. Drain and rinse with cool water to stop the noodles from overcooking. Add the noodles to the vegetables in the bowl. Toss with the soy sauce dressing.

Thinly slice the chicken against the grain and place on top of the noodle mixture. Sprinkle green onions over the top and serve. (The salad can be stored in an airtight container for up to 2 days.)

Serves: 4 | Serving Size: 2 cups noodle mixture plus 4 oz chicken

Mexican Burrito with Chicken & Avocado

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Why buy burritos when they’re so simple to make? Save time with precooked chicken (you can also use leftovers!) and budget-friendly staples like canned beans, shredded cheese, salsa and frozen corn. Then, step up your burrito game by adding creamy avocado and fresh lime juice. Wrap in foil, and pack for a homemade lunch.

Active time: 15 minutes | Total time: 20 minutes

Mexican Burrito with Chicken & Avocado

Ingredients

  • 8 oz (227g) boneless, skinless chicken breasts, cooked 
  • 1/4 cup (38g) fresh or frozen corn (thawed)
  • 1/4 cup (43g) no salt added black beans, drained and rinsed
  • 2 tbsp finely chopped cilantro, divided (plus more for optional garnish)
  • 1/2 cup (56g) shredded reduced-sodium Cheddar cheese
  • 1 small avocado (150g), diced and divided
  • 1/4 cup (60g) low-sodium tomatillo salsa, divided (plus more for optional garnish)
  • 1 tsp fresh lime juice
  • 1/4 tsp salt (or to taste)
  • 1/4 tsp pepper (or to taste)
  • 3 medium (8-inch/45g each) whole-wheat flour tortillas
  • Diced tomatoes (optional garnish)

Directions

Warm the chicken (if desired) and chop into bite-size pieces.

In a small bowl, combine the chicken, corn, black beans, 1 tbsp cilantro, cheese, 1/2 avocado and 2 tbsp salsa. Mix well to combine. Add salt and pepper. In a blender or food processor, combine the lime juice and the remaining avocado, salsa and cilantro. Blend until creamy. Taste for seasoning, adding more lime juice or salt as needed.

Spread 1/3 of the chicken filling into the center of a flour tortilla. Fold in the sides, and bring up the bottom of the tortilla, tucking in the ends. Keep rolling, pressing and creasing the end of the tortilla as you finish rolling it up. Top with avocado cream, and, if desired, more salsa, cilantro and tomatoes.

Serves: 3 | Serving Size: 1 burrito

Nutrition Bonus: Potassium: 535mg; Iron: 20%; Vitamin A: 10%; Vitamin C: 25%; Calcium: 25%

Do athletes need to be mentally tough or mentally pliable?

Bob Bowman (Image: WSJ)

Legendary swimming coach Bob Bowman, who has worked with some of the world’s greatest swimmers, including Michael Phelps, Léon Marchand and Summer McIntosh, recently attracted considerable attention with an unusual statement on social media (see here): he does not want his athletes to be mentally tough! Instead, Bowman argues that mentally tough athletes can become rigid and break down under pressure, particularly when events do not unfold as planned. He wants athletes to be mentally pliable—able to adapt quickly, remain focused and perform effectively regardless of the circumstances.

To illustrate his point, Bowman reflected on the preparations for two Olympic Games. Before Athens 2004, concerns arose because the swimming pool had no roof, leaving athletes to compete in extreme heat. Similarly, ahead of Beijing 2008, air pollution and smog caused widespread concern. Yet both competitions went ahead, and athletes still delivered outstanding performances. For Bowman, what separated those who succeeded from those who struggled was not necessarily mental toughness, but the ability to adapt to the unexpected. The best athletes maintained their concentration, made the necessary adjustments and refused to let circumstances beyond their control undermine their performance.

Bowman’s message is simple: when things do not go your way, or plans change at the last minute, do not waste energy complaining. Assess the situation, make the necessary adjustments and get on with it.

Does this challenge one of sport’s most widely accepted beliefs? We constantly hear about the importance of developing mentally tough athletes, but is mental toughness overrated? Should coaches place greater emphasis on developing mentally pliable athletes who can adapt to whatever challenges come their way?

Can AI predict injuries in professional rugby?

(Image: Framesports)

A recent study analysed 129 male professional rugby players from the four Irish provincial teams — Munster, Leinster, Connacht and Ulster — across three seasons from 2021–2022 to 2023–2024. The researchers collected a huge amount of information, including GPS workload data, wellness questionnaires, musculoskeletal screening, player characteristics and injury history. In total, the dataset contained over 141,000 player-day records, including more than 47,000 training exposures and 5,700 match exposures.

Interestingly, even with all this information, AI was poor at predicting whether a player would sustain a non-contact lower-limb injury in their next training session or match. During a nine-month test period, the best-performing AI model correctly predicted just 8 of 54 injuries. Even more strikingly, it produced 636 high-risk alerts — meaning there were approximately 77 false alarms for every injury it correctly predicted.

So, even with huge amounts of athlete-monitoring data and AI, predicting whether a player will get injured in their next training session or match remains extraordinarily difficult.

Imagining squats without doing them could boost cognition

(Image: ChatGPT)

A new study published in Frontiers in Cognition found that athletes can enhance certain aspects of cognitive function by imagining themselves performing an exercise rather than actually doing it. The study involved 98 athletes divided into three groups: one group imagined performing squats for 30 minutes, another group physically performed squats, and the control group sat quietly for 30 minutes.

Interestingly, the group that engaged in mental imagery of squats performed better on various computer-based tests assessing cognitive functions such as decision-making, memory, attention, and reaction speed. These results highlight the potential effectiveness of mental imagery and suggest it could enhance cognitive readiness without the physical fatigue associated with actual exercise.

Cognitive abilities like decision-making, memory, attention, and reaction speed are crucial in many sports. However, the study does not determine whether improved performance on these computer-based tests translates to better sporting performance. For instance, it does not clarify whether a football player who uses mental imagery before a match will be able to spot a pass more quickly, react faster to an opponent, or make more effective decisions. This is an important question for future research to explore.

The danger of chasing perfection

Michael Owen & Jonny Wilkinson (Image: High Performance Podcast)

The High Performance Podcast recently featured former England footballer Michael Owen, and a clip shared on Instagram sparked a fascinating debate about the potential dangers of chasing perfection.

In the clip, Owen discussed former England rugby star Jonny Wilkinson and his relentless approach to practice. According to Owen, Wilkinson would line up 20 balls and try to kick all 20 successfully. If he missed even one, however, he would reset the 20 balls and start again from ball one. Owen admitted that Wilkinson’s mentality fascinated him because it was completely different from his own. Although he acknowledged that this mindset can certainly help an athlete reach the top, he also described it as “pretty damn dangerous.”

Although Owen was fascinated by this relentless pursuit of perfection, he knew that such a mentality would never have worked for him. Instead, he described himself as having what he called a “shit filter.” If he had a bad touch during a game, rather than dwelling on it, he would tell himself: “That’s my bad touch out the way.” This allowed him to move on immediately while continuing to believe that he could be the best player on the pitch.

This clip from The High Performance Podcast is particularly thought-provoking because it highlights two very different approaches to elite performance. Jonny Wilkinson and Michael Owen both reached the highest level of their respective sports, yet they appeared to have very different relationships with mistakes and perfection.

Social media before training: a performance killer?

(Image: Women’s Health)

Lars Johansen, a sports psychologist at Denmark’s national high-performance organisation, recently warned that using social media before exercise can cause mental fatigue and impair physical performance (see here). Johansen highlighted a study showing that participants who used social media for 30 minutes before taking a Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Test performed approximately 10% worse than a control group that did not.

The study Johansen referenced examined young football players after 30 minutes of cognitively demanding smartphone use. In the test, players covered an average of 1,610 m compared to 1,780 m in the control condition—a 10.56% reduction in running distance. However, the task was not simply scrolling through Instagram or TikTok; it involved a specifically designed, cognitively demanding smartphone application.

Nevertheless, Johansen likened the physical decline from pre-exercise social media use to “smoking three to four cigarettes before you train.” While acknowledging that a total ban on social media is unrealistic, he strongly advises against using it in the hours leading up to training and competition, when peak physical and mental performance is required.

While the study Johansen cited may be more nuanced than everyday scrolling, his core message about social media’s impact on athletic performance has never been more relevant. Are female athletes underfuelled and underhydrated?

(Image: Axes Physical Theraphy)

Recent research from the Gatorade Sports Science Institute has revealed some concerning findings regarding the nutrition and hydration habits of female athletes. The research included over 500 female athletes across various sports and found that 43% of participants began their training in a dehydrated state. Additionally, most athletes failed to sufficiently replenish the sodium they lost while sweating, and over half weren’t taking in enough carbohydrates to support their performance.

These results are troubling and suggest that many female athletes may not be performing at their full potential due to poor hydration, insufficient sodium intake, and inadequate carbohydrate consumption. The researchers also recommend establishing female-specific hydration guidelines instead of relying on data primarily derived from male athletes. By developing female-specific sweat-rate classifications, awareness can be raised about the importance of individualised hydration and sodium replacement strategies for female athletes.

It is important to note that this research was conducted by the Gatorade Sports Science Institute, which may introduce some bias due to industry affiliation. However, the research highlights a significant issue and emphasises the need for greater focus on hydration and fuelling practices among female athletes.

Advice for GLP-1 Users

📅 Oct 02, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Someone asked me what I’d recommend to someone taking GLP drugs. I’ve written posts and newsletters on this before, but there’s one newer bit of advice I haven’t mentioned before: eat more.

The thing with these drugs is that they work incredibly well. Whether it’s semaglutide (Ozempic), tirzepatide (Mounjaro), or the yet-to-be-FDA-approved retatrutide, these compounds are very good at getting you to eat less. They have other effects, too, like slowed gastric emptying, “slower” digestion, and others—some positive, some more negative.

But the one thing almost everyone reports is that you eat far less food on these things.

Subscribe now

That can be good if you need it. Most people eat too much. Even if it’s healthy food. I remember being at PrimalCon back in the day and being shocked by the amount of food people could put away. Everything was high quality, cooked in good fats, well-sourced, but people would routinely report that they gained weight at PrimalCon. Food is food. Volume is volume. And that’s why these drugs are having such powerful effects in the overweight and obese. They’re losing weight because they aren’t eating very much and, most importantly, they aren’t hungry doing it.

But what about when you start losing too much? When you start losing muscle, losing energy?

Everyone says the obvious: “make your calories count.” Get your micronutrients. Make it high quality, whole foods. With less volume coming in, the quality of what little volume you do get becomes increasingly important.

And they talk about protein, too, rightfully. A big part of countering the negative effects of GLP drugs, like muscle loss, requires that you stay active. Lift weights, and support that activity by eating protein.

These are all good, but you’ve heard them before.

The one piece of advice you might not have heard, that sounds a bit unconventional, but that becomes useful especially as you start approaching your ideal weight: eat more.

Try to eat a bit more than you feel like.

Many issues we see with these drugs are from undernourishment. You’re running a deficit and, depending on the drug in question, burning more energy. You need to eat a little more than you think.

It’ll still be “less,” don’t worry.

Colostrum vs. Collagen: Which One Should You Take?

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A surprisingly common query I get is colostrum vs. collagen—which is better? Maybe it’s the fact that they both start with the same three letters. Maybe it’s that they’re both alternative health darlings currently enjoying a huge uptick in interest.

Subscribe now

Whatever the reason for their connection in people’s minds, I thought it would be a good idea to do a quick write-up on how colostrum and collagen might interact in the body, what the relative benefits are, and whether you can take them both together.

Let’s dive right in.

Where Do Colostrum and Collagen Benefits Overlap?

Contrary to what the influencers might tell you, there isn’t a ton of research showing that bovine colostrum has a wide range of benefits for adults. It’s definitely helpful for newborns, since their guts are incredibly permeable, and colostrum is literally the first food they’re supposed to eat. Even cow colostrum has been shown to improve outcomes for newborns and premature infants. No arguments there.

But I’d caution against expecting too much from colostrum supplements. The best case for adding it to your diet as an adult is improving gut health, reducing intestinal permeability, and improving immune function—particularly for athletes.

Colostrum has been heavily researched for its ability to reduce gut permeability in various populations, including athletes, medical patients, and everyday folks.1-4 People who take bovine colostrum supplements often report better gut health and a more intact gut lining. Zonulin, a gut compound that increases permeability, frequently drops with colostrum intake.

Researchers aren’t entirely sure how bovine colostrum benefits the gut. Some people claim it regulates specific growth factors in the gut lining that help repair and support its structure. That’s colostrum’s primary role in infancy: improving gut viability so the immune system can develop, enabling digestion of food instead of relying on the womb for sustenance. Thanks in large part to colostrum benefits, this new member of the human race is able to handle what the world throws at it and develop a healthy immune response and gut lining

The challenge is that the adult gut isn’t as permeable as a newborn’s by design. For instance, the IGF-1 in bovine colostrum isn’t absorbed or utilized by adults.5

Colostrum can also regulate inflammation and help maintain iron balance in athletes, largely due to its lactoferrin content.6 Lactoferrin is an immune protein that improves iron absorption while reducing free iron—an inflammatory agent—circulating in the body. Athletes taking colostrum for instance see higher spikes in inflammation during workouts but lower resting inflammation, showing it promotes “smart” inflammation.

Meanwhile, collagen benefits for gut health are much more direct. Collagen provides the actual building blocks—amino acids like glycine—for repairing and growing the gut lining. Whether it turns out that colostrum does indeed trigger growth factors that help the gut repair itself (by no means confirmed), or if it just lowers inflammation via lactoferrin which allows the repair to proceed, you’ll need extra collagen to make those repairs.

Colostrum assists with gut repair while collagen provides a substrate required for the repair to occur.

And so the two compounds are not competing with each other, but potentially complementary. Both colostrum and collagen improve gut health, enhance skin health and appearance, and boost immune function by different but complementary routes.

Can You Take Them Together?

Yes, you can absolutely take collagen and colostrum together. In fact, I’d argue that if colostrum works, they’re almost made for each other. You don’t need to mix them in the same shake or take them simultaneously; just include both in your routine for maximum benefit.

Adding both colostrum and collagen supplements can create a synergistic effect, particularly for gut and immune health. Collagen serves as the substrate, while colostrum provides potential activation via growth factors.

When Is One More Useful Than the Other?

It depends on your dietary needs. If you only take one, take collagen. Collagen is like the fourth macronutrient, providing glycine—an amino acid we no longer get enough of in modern diets. This makes collagen essential for maintaining skin, bones, blood vessels, and more. It’s also widely beneficial, whether you’re looking for joint health, anti-aging, or gut support.

If you regularly consume foods like bone broth, gelatinous meats, or stews, you may already be getting plenty of collagen. In that case, colostrum supplements could add more value, especially for individuals with immune or gut issues that dietary changes haven’t resolved.

Colostrum, derived from the mature milk of cows after giving birth, is essentially absent from the diet unless you specifically seek it out. It’s best suited for people dealing with gut inflammation or immune dysregulation. Athletes might benefit from colostrum’s ability to regulate iron and inflammation, while others may see improved resistance to respiratory infections.

Here’s How I Break It Down:

Who should take collagen?

Everyone.

Who should take colostrum?

  • Athletes with iron balance issues.

  • Adults prone to upper respiratory tract infections.

  • Individuals with leaky gut or gut inflammation.

If you’re on a budget, you can likely replicate most of the colostrum supplement benefits with a high-quality whey isolate (which contains some lactoferrin, but not as much as colostrum) and a dedicated lactoferrin supplement. My guess is that lactoferrin is doing much of the heavy lifting for colostrum’s benefits.

Final Thoughts on Colostrum vs. Collagen

Both colostrum and collagen offer unique benefits, but collagen stands out as the more universally essential supplement. Its role in collagen production, gut lining repair, and overall health makes it indispensable for most people. Bovine colostrum can complement collagen, especially in specific scenarios, but it isn’t as necessary or widely applicable.

For most, collagen is the clear winner. Still, if you have unique needs or simply want to experiment with bovine colostrum benefits, adding both to your regimen can offer the best of both worlds.

Are cooking skills the missing ingredient in sports nutrition?

(Image: My Fitness Pal)

Sports nutrition has advanced considerably in recent years, and athletes now routinely receive expert guidance to support training and performance. However, a new review highlights an often-overlooked question: do athletes know how to prepare and cook their food?

The review examined previous interventions designed to improve athletes’ food preparation and cooking skills. Despite extensive research on sports nutrition, the researchers found only 10 studies that combined hands-on food preparation with nutrition education. None measured whether athletes’ practical cooking skills improved.

The review therefore highlights a gap between sports nutrition knowledge and practical application. For example, a field-sport athlete may know to consume sufficient carbohydrate before training and combine carbohydrate with high-quality protein post-training. Yet this knowledge has limited value if they lack the confidence or skills to shop for, prepare, and cook suitable meals.

The review raises an important question for coaches and sports nutritionists: are we spending too much time telling athletes what to eat and too little teaching them how to prepare it?

New research challenges osteoarthritis exercise advice

(Image: Chris Bailey Orthopaedics)

A massive longitudinal study has challenged conventional medical advice for people with knee or hip osteoarthritis. Traditionally, these patients have been encouraged to choose low-impact activities such as cycling or swimming to help protect their joints. However, this large-scale longitudinal study analysed more than 17,000 patients and produced some very interesting findings.

The researchers found that participating in high-impact activities was not associated with an increased risk of knee replacement. In fact, those who regularly engaged in high-impact activities had a substantially lower risk of requiring a knee replacement than those who did not. Therefore, if an individual’s capacity allows, people with knee or hip osteoarthritis should not fear high-impact activities. Instead, they should embrace them, as this longitudinal study suggests they may help support long-term joint health.

New research on HYROX injuries

(Image: Chilitri)

As HYROX continues to grow rapidly in popularity, the first prospective study tracking injuries and health problems among HYROX athletes has now been published. The study followed 89 athletes over a 12-week period, providing new insight into the types of injuries occurring and the areas of the body most commonly affected.

The researchers recorded 14.4 injuries per 1,000 hours of training and competition. Notably, 73% of injuries were classified as overuse rather than acute injuries, while the knee was the most commonly affected area, accounting for 22% of all injuries. Running was also the most frequently reported injury mechanism. Interestingly, shoulder injuries were almost non-existent, despite shoulder problems being relatively common in other high-intensity training environments.

While considerably more research is needed, these early findings highlight the substantial running demands associated with HYROX and suggest that managing running load may be particularly important. For coaches and athletes, gradually building running capacity and carefully monitoring training load could therefore help reduce the risk of overuse injuries.

Why Mikel Arteta is deliberately disrupting Arsenal’s preparation

(Image: The Independent)

Arsenal F.C. manager Mikel Arteta has sparked significant online discussion after revealing an unconventional method he used during pre-season to deliberately disrupt his players’ usual preparation (see here). Arteta said he intentionally had the team arrive late to the stadium, delayed meals, made the dressing room uncomfortably hot and even reduced the number of massage beds available. These planned disruptions interfered with the players’ normal pre-match routines, with Arteta believing that exposing players and staff to these situations could help them become more adaptable when unexpected challenges arise during the season.

While this approach may seem unusual, it closely resembles a psychological technique known as stress exposure training. This involves exposing individuals to controlled and realistic stressors, so they become more accustomed to handling pressure and disruption when similar situations occur for real.

Typically, coaches strive to create the best possible environment for their athletes to prepare and perform. However, Arteta’s approach presents an interesting alternative. Occasionally introducing controlled stress and disruption into the training environment may help athletes become more adaptable and better prepared to cope when things do not go according to plan.

How the Dallas Cowboys are using sports science to beat the heat

(Image: Studio Outside)

Earlier this month, a compelling story emerged about how the Dallas Cowboys, an American football team, are navigating the challenges posed by extreme weather (see here). The Dallas area had gone nearly two months without any measurable rainfall and was facing sweltering heat, prompting the team’s sports science and medical staff to raise concerns about players’ hydration during training sessions.

To address these issues, the Cowboys have gone beyond just monitoring temperature and weather conditions. They have implemented sweat testing and installed specialised urinals that evaluate players’ hydration levels when they urinate. Armed with this data, the sports science and medical staff can better assess whether players are fit to train outdoors in the extreme heat and humidity or if it’s wiser to hold the session indoors.

This situation underscores the importance of looking beyond just the weather when deciding on outdoor training in extreme heat. It also highlights the significance of understanding how each athlete is responding to these conditions, enabling coaches and medical staff to ensure that players are adequately hydrated and ready to handle the rigours of training in such intense heat.

Coaching tips you don’t get on social media!

James de Lacey (Image: Canvas Rebel)

Our very own James de Lacey recently shared some excellent practical coaching tips on LinkedIn for delivering team training sessions more effectively.

Firstly, when addressing players, ensure that all of them are positioned in front of you rather than behind you. This means avoiding the common practice of having players form a circle around the coach. By positioning everyone in front, it becomes much easier to communicate clearly with the entire group.

Secondly, when communicating with players outdoors, take into account the position of the sun. Players should not have to look directly into the sun while receiving instructions. Instead, the coach should face the sun, even if it feels slightly uncomfortable, rather than expecting the entire team to do so. With the sun behind them, players will be in a better position to focus, listen, and absorb instructions.

Lastly, there shouldn’t be a noticeable divide between where the warm-up ends and training begins. Excessive stretching and dynamic movements should be completed by players in their own time before training, allowing the session to flow naturally into the main training activities.

Exercise Tech to Really Get You Moving

📅 Sep 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

People ask me all the time about exercise tech. Do I have any tools, gadgets, gizmos, or devices I’m using or would recommend? After all, the world of fitness tech has exploded. There are things you can strap on, attach to your body, loop around your wrist, or wear on your finger that all claim to make getting fit easier and more enjoyable (and more trackable).

I don’t see the point. If I ever need a device to tell me if I had a great workout or if I’m sleepy or not, it’s time to throw in the towel.

You can spend $1000 on a smartwatch with a $30 a month subscription fee plus Strava, plus Peloton workout subscription, plus the new model every year, plus the smart ring, plus the heart rate variability monitor, plus the meditation app subscription to help you get that HRV back up… or you could spend good money on a high-quality piece of equipment that will last forever and make training enjoyable again.

These are my picks.

Get a good watercraft.

Maybe it’s a kayak. Maybe it’s a standup paddle board. Maybe it’s a surfboard or windsurfing rig. Most people reading this will have some level of water access—and I urge you all to avail yourselves of it. There’s nothing quite so Primal and human as traversing a natural body of water. Spend as much as you’re comfortable, and don’t be afraid to buy used.

My personal recommendation is a standup paddleboard from Starboard. Hard board, if you have the storage for it.

Get a good adventure bike.

I say “adventure bike” because while you can take a mountain or fat tire bike out on the road, you can’t take a road bike on the trails or sand. I know, I know, riding a mountain bike or fat tire bike on the road isn’t “optimal” or “efficient,” but this is all about fun, and having a bike that can go almost everywhere is far more conducive to fun than a bike designed for only one specific type of environment.

I’m partial to Trek for both mountain and fat tire bikes. I have a Trek Farley I use twice a week minimum on Miami Beach, and it’s probably my favorite bike I’ve ever owned. And yes, it works on the street.

Get a good weight vest or rucksack.

For as long as hominids have been bipeds, we’ve been supporting heavy loads and walking for miles underneath them. A million years ago, it was the haunch of an antelope we successfully stole from a larger predator thrown over the shoulder. 2000 years ago, it was 50 pounds of armor, weapons, and personal affects piled on our backs on some campaign in the Mediterranean. Whether you were a farmer, explorer, naturalist, prospector, soldier, or parent, you were carrying heavy things while walking across the environment.

Some call this rucking, some call it loaded carries. The point is that we are designed to carry heavy loads and walk. It’s the entire reason why we have opposable thumbs (for grabbing) and bipedalism (to free up our hands and give us an upright posture that can better support a load).

I recommend GoRuck if you want to ruck and Aduro if you want a weight vest. They go up to 30 pounds, so if you want heavier, I’d go with Rogue.

A gym membership.

For the price of a high end fitness watch, Peloton bike, fancy treadmill, indoor rower, and all the other doodads you want, with monthly subscriptions for all of them, you could find a great gym with a sauna, pool, cold plunge, extensive free and machine weight collection and cardio equipment and, most importantly, a pool of interesting members you can socialize with. I’ve met many great friends and business partners at the gym, and they’re often what kept me coming back day in and day out.

Get good footwear.

Footwear is everything. It’s how we interact with the world and how we generate force—by pushing off to start the battle against gravity. No matter what exercise or sport or activity you’re doing, your kinetic chain starts with the feet. If you’re starting with suboptimal footwear, you’re short-changing yourself.

I recommend Peluva, of course—my own footwear brand that I built from the ground up to be the closest approximation to actually going barefoot. I’d love it if we could go barefoot everywhere, but the modern world we’ve built simply doesn’t allow it. Most of the surfaces we walk, run, and move across aren’t meant for bare feet.

A New Strike Against High Protein?

📅 Sep 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A recent paper is fueling the plant-based crowd. In it, the authors found that protein restriction turned off many genetic and molecular signals of aging.

It was a synthesis of over 350 existing studies, a mix of in vitro research, animal research, and human trials, and the overall finding was that restricting protein elicited favorable levels and changes to mTOR, FGF21, oxidative stress, and other things associated with or contributory to aging.

Subscribe now

Now, the study had some interesting things, right? There was some nuance there. They readily admitted that protein restriction acts differently in sedentary versus active individuals. But overall, the thrust of the paper, and what most people will take away from it, and what your vegan friends are no doubt bragging to you about, is that less protein is better for longevity.

Does Eating Meat Reduce Longevity?

Much of the research rests on the assumption that specific amino acids in excess promote aging. The one they say most is methionine. It’s found in dairy, muscle meat, eggs, and other animal products, and absolutely, excessive amounts of it can promote aging in animal models, and likely humans. No argument there.

But what these studies never explore is the reality that methionine is balanced by another amino acid: glycine. Glycine is almost entirely absent from the classic animal protein products like meat or eggs or dairy, and it’s found almost exclusively in connective tissue, bones, and skin.

There is a classic study where they put rodents on a high-methionine diet, and sure enough, those rats died pretty young. But they took another group and gave them glycine, same amount of methionine, just with extra glycine, and those guys lived long, healthy lives, just as long as the rodents who lived on a restricted-methionine diet.1-2

We see echoes of this in the human literature. Tons of research finds that low glycine levels in the serum predict type 2 diabetes, heart disease, and many of the other diseases classically linked to meat consumption.

Now let’s look at Hong Kong, which is one of the longest-living populations on Earth, who also happen to eat the most meat of anyone on Earth. But the meat they’re eating is not just steaks and burgers and roasts. They’re also eating skin, snouts, feet, and other animal foods that are high in glycine.

This is why collagen is so essential for the modern meat eater who doesn’t want to eat the obscure stuff like skin and bones. Thirty to 40 grams of collagen a day provides enough glycine to balance out all the muscle meat and other animal protein you eat. And although we don’t have direct human trials of balancing out methionine with glycine and looking at lifespan, the circumstantial evidence is pretty strong that it’s going to have good effects.

Except that in the real world, when you see people who actually practice this, these are people who live this lifestyle, they’ve read the studies, they’ve convinced themselves that protein restriction is key to living long. They are generally pretty unwell. They’re frail, always cold, have low energy, and generally aren’t the picture of health we are imagining for ourselves.

Now, they may be better off and look better than the general population in some ways. They tend to be thinner, at least, and, you know, even their biomarkers look pretty good. But I refuse to believe that this is the path to optimal health. You may limp by a bit longer. You may slowly walk into old age, but can you still run hills? Can you play? Are you strong?

And that’s where the study loses me.

My Beef with the Findings

Nothing against the authors themselves. It’s pretty impossible to cover every angle, and as I said before, they did a pretty fair job of noting that active exercisers have a higher tolerance of protein. But the true path to health is on the teeter-totter, the seesaw, the waxing and the waning.

It’s a big steak after a workout and then maybe skipping breakfast the next day and just having some fish with salad for lunch. It’s periods of abundance and periods of restriction. It is high-protein days and lower-protein days. We see this everywhere. We see it in wild animals, predators who eat and gorge themselves for a day or two and then go for several days without eating anything. You see this in the fasting habits of monks and other religious men. Christianity even has protein fasting built into it.

No, I’m not saying that you should fast for three days after you eat a big steak like a tiger or wolf, or eliminate protein for a 14-day period. I’m just saying that the world is cyclical. That seems embedded in our environment and also our biology.

This is why, over the years, I have settled on not daily protein targets, but four- to five-day protein targets, even weekly protein targets. I want to eat an average of 120 grams of protein a day over the course of a week. Some days it’ll be 180, other days it’ll be 70. It doesn’t matter so much day-to-day as it does across the week.

Eccentric training: strength and flexibility combined?

(Image: Steel Supplements)

A very interesting and cleverly designed new study has shown that eccentric training may improve flexibility just as effectively as static stretching. Participants completed a 10-week intervention, training three times per week. The researchers used a within-subject design, whereby one leg performed eccentric-only hamstring training while the other performed conventional static hamstring stretching. This meant that each participant effectively acted as their own control.

The results showed similar improvements in joint range of motion following both interventions. However, the eccentric-trained leg also experienced significantly greater increases in hamstring strength and muscle size, highlighting an important additional benefit of eccentric training.

Therefore, while both methods appear effective for improving hamstring flexibility, eccentric training may offer coaches and athletes a more time-efficient approach by simultaneously targeting flexibility, strength and muscle size.

Is HYROX more about running than strength?

(Image: Velites)

Dr Stephen Patterson, Professor in Applied Exercise Physiology and Performance, recently published a highly informative post on HYROX on LinkedIn. In the post, Patterson outlines the key findings from recent research examining performance in HYROX.

The research analysed HYROX performance across seven competitive seasons and nearly 40,000 individual PRO and ELITE race results. The findings show that performance has improved considerably as HYROX has developed. Among the top 100 performers, race times improved by approximately 13 minutes in the men’s field and 17 minutes in the women’s field from Season 1 to Season 7. Interestingly, much of this improvement came from faster running, with running times improving by approximately eight minutes in men and 10 minutes in women.

However, Patterson also highlights that poor performance in the strength-based stations can disproportionately increase overall finishing time, particularly among slower athletes. Therefore, while running ability appears to be a major driver of HYROX performance, athletes still need sufficient strength to avoid losing significant time during the loaded stations. The challenge appears to be finding the right balance: developing enough strength to perform these stations efficiently, without adding unnecessary muscle mass that could potentially compromise running performance.

AiKYNETIX’s new tennis serve analysis

(Image: AiKYNETIX)

With the US Open currently ongoing, the timing of our next bulletin seems very apt. AiKYNETIX, which has featured in SFS Weekly in the past (see here), has made a breakthrough in the tennis world with its new tennis serve analysis.

The tennis serve is a highly complex movement that requires the coordinated transfer of energy through the entire kinetic chain, from the lower body through the pelvis, trunk and shoulder before eventually reaching the racket. Traditionally, gaining a detailed understanding of this sequence has often required sophisticated biomechanics equipment and expertise.

However, AiKYNETIX’s new tennis serve feature breaks the serve down into different biomechanical phases based on established frameworks. Rather than analysing the serve as one complete movement, the system can examine what is happening at different stages of the serve. This phase-based approach may help coaches better understand how an athlete transfers movement through the kinetic chain. For example, the analysis can examine the transition from lower-body contribution into pelvic and trunk movement, before progressing through the shoulder and upper limb and eventually into racket action and ball contact.

Another interesting development is the ability to generate detailed analysis reports directly from the platform. This could help bridge the gap between collecting biomechanical information and actually using it within the coaching environment.

So, if you are working in the tennis industry, then it is definitely worth checking out AiKYNETIX’s new tennis serve feature. Its real potential may lie in making detailed biomechanical analysis more accessible and practical within everyday coaching environments. Does Neutonic enhance productivity?

(Image: Chloe Hamard)

Neutonic, the productivity drink created by influencers Chris Williamson and James Smith, has experienced a remarkable rise in popularity and is quickly becoming a staple on shelves in shops and markets worldwide. But does it truly enhance productivity?

Shortly after its launch, nutritionist and performance coach Adam McDonald shared a well-rounded video review of the drink. Although this video dates back over two years, it remains a hidden gem, offering an expert perspective that many would benefit from revisiting.

In his review, McDonald points out a key advantage of Neutonic: the ingredients list clearly states the exact doses of each component, unlike many products that hide this information in proprietary blends. This transparency allows consumers to understand what they’re consuming. He further dives into each ingredient and the science supporting them. For example, Cognizin may improve memory and attention, but the evidence is limited, particularly among healthy adults aged 20 to 40, the drink’s target market. Rhodiola Rosea shows promising evidence for reducing mental fatigue and boosting cognitive performance under stress. The combination of caffeine and L-Theanine enhances alertness and reaction times while mitigating the jitteriness and anxiety often associated with caffeine alone. Although Panax Ginseng might improve reaction time, the evidence for this is highly inconsistent. Lastly, B Vitamins play a vital role in brain function, but they are unlikely to provide a direct productivity boost unless someone is deficient.

While McDonald acknowledges that Neutonic has some scientific backing for its ingredient selection and is better formulated than many energy drinks, he emphasises that prioritising sleep, improving time management, and managing distractions will likely yield far greater benefits for productivity than consuming Neutonic. Considering that Neutonic comes with a relatively high price tag compared to other energy drinks, and any boosts in focus and attention are likely to be subtle, it may be wiser to explore other strategies for enhancing productivity rather than relying on a can of Neutonic.
