The future of protein supplementation?

(Image: ChatGPT)

PlaybookHQ, a leading YouTube channel covering sports science and technology, recently explored a potentially exciting development in protein supplementation: Space Milk, a novel yeast-derived protein supplement (see here).

The product is made from yeast protein extracted through fermentation. Space Milk describes it as the “perfect protein”, claiming that it provides a complete amino acid profile, is easy to digest and offers an alternative to conventional protein supplements.

The video highlights potential limitations of existing protein sources, including incomplete amino acid profiles in certain plant-based proteins, concerns about heavy metal contamination in some supplements and digestive issues associated with dairy-based whey protein. Space Milk claims to address these limitations and emphasises transparency through independent third-party testing and publicly available results.

Although the concept is intriguing, further independent research is needed to substantiate claims about its digestibility, protein quality and effects on muscle protein synthesis, as well as any potential advantages over established protein sources such as whey.

It will be fascinating to see whether yeast-derived protein supplements prove to be a genuine innovation in sports nutrition or simply another short-lived industry trend.

Why are FC Bayern Munich players wearing tape on their ears?

(Image: Facebook)

Recent images of FC Bayern Munich players wearing adhesive tape on their earlobes have attracted considerable attention on social media (see here), with many people wondering why the tape is there. However, the practice is not new: FC Bayern Munich players were also spotted wearing similar tape last season.

The explanation lies in the club’s sports science and performance-monitoring practices. FC Bayern Munich’s medical and sports science staff collect blood lactate samples from players’ earlobes instead of using the more traditional fingertip method. These samples allow staff to measure blood lactate concentrations and assess players’ physiological responses to training. The resulting data can help staff tailor training intensity, monitor responses to physical workloads and adjust where necessary.

So, there is a scientific explanation for the adhesive tape seen on FC Bayern Munich players’ ears. It is not a new fashion trend nor evidence that all the players suddenly decided to have their ears pierced at the same time!

Young athletes don’t develop every physical quality at the same rate

(Image: Premier League)

A recently published longitudinal meta-analysis examined patterns of physical development in male adolescent athletes. Drawing on 28 studies across nine sports, the researchers focused on three key physical qualities: lower-body power, acceleration and endurance. Lower-body power was assessed primarily through the countermovement jump, acceleration through the 10-metre sprint, and endurance through tests including the Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Test, the 20-metre shuttle run, and interval shuttle runs.

Notably, improvements in lower-body power and endurance were not linear. The fastest rates of improvement occurred at approximately 13.2 years for lower-body power and 13.8 years for endurance, broadly coinciding with the typical timing of peak height velocity in male athletes. Yet acceleration followed a different pattern. Although it is tempting to assume that every physical quality improves rapidly as young athletes enter puberty, 10-metre sprint performance improved relatively steadily between the ages of 12 and 17, with no clearly identifiable surge. Acceleration, therefore, did not appear to benefit from the same obvious maturational boost as lower-body power and endurance.

The key message from this research is that young athletes do not develop every physical quality at the same rate. Around peak height velocity, qualities such as lower-body power and endurance may improve rapidly, while others, including acceleration, may develop more steadily throughout adolescence.

In this article on the Winter Arc, we are focusing on how GLP-1 users can borrow the “lock in” idea without turning it into restriction. You will learn how to build seasonal habits around protein, strength training, movement, and realistic tracking through the colder months and holiday season.

What the Winter Arc Means for GLP-1 Users

The Winter Arc is often used to describe a three-month period, usually October through December, focused on goals before the new year. Merriam-Webster describes it as a time of focus and discipline to get a head start on self-improvement goals.  (1)

That idea can be useful. The end of the year can feel busy and unpredictable. Travel, holiday meals, colder weather, and shorter days can make routines harder to keep.

“A seasonal reset can help you decide what matters before January arrives. But there is a catch: “locking in” can easily turn into all-or-nothing thinking,” says Katherine Basbaum MS RD, MyFitnessPal dietitian.

For GLP-1 users, that can be especially tricky. Some days may feel different than others because of appetite, digestion, energy, or medication timing — nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, stomach pain, fatigue, and dizziness are all things people commonly deal with. (2,6)

That does not mean you cannot build momentum. It just means your Winter Arc should have guardrails.

Think of it this way: your Winter Arc is not a punishment plan. It is a support plan.

How To Lock In Without Going Extreme

A GLP-1 Winter Arc works best when it is simple enough to repeat.

Instead of choosing 10 habits and trying to perfect all of them, choose a few habits that support your bigger goal. The best winter habits are the ones you can still do on a cold Tuesday, after a busy workday, or during a holiday week.

Start with the minimum version

A minimum version is the low-pressure version of a habit. It might be shorter, simpler, or less detailed than your ideal plan, but it still keeps you connected to the habit. This helps you keep momentum without turning one imperfect day into a reason to stop.

Build in flexible days

Some days may not be ideal for a hard workout or three balanced meals. That does not mean your plan is broken.

If you feel unusually tired, nauseated, dizzy, or under-fueled, choose the gentler version. Take a walk. Stretch. Eat one good meal and a protein-rich snack or two if that feels manageable. 

Habit 1: Make Strength Training Your Anchor

Strength training on GLP-1 does not need to mean intense gym workouts. A good rule of thumb is to work in strength training at least twice a week, hitting the major muscle groups — legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders, and arms.  (3)

This fits the Winter Arc well because strength training gives you a clear habit to repeat. You can do it at a gym or at home with simple equipment like resistance bands or dumbbells.

A beginner-friendly session might include:

  • Chair squats or sit-to-stands
  • Wall push-ups or incline push-ups
  • Resistance band rows
  • Glute bridges
  • Step-ups
  • Light carries with dumbbells or grocery bags

Start with one to three sets of each move. Keep the effort moderate. You should finish feeling like you could come back and do it again later in the week. You can connect with a personal trainer for a personalized routine.

This is where the muscle-loss conversation naturally comes in, but it’s not the whole story. “Choosing strength training as one of your Winter Arc goals will definitely help towards maintaining and building muscle tissue, but that’s not the only benefit,” says Basbaum. “You’ll also see gains in your functional fitness, which means lower risk of injury and improvement in quality of life.” 

Still, muscle matters. It supports strength, balance, daily movement, and feeling capable in your body.

One recent review looked at people using GLP-1 medications at weight-loss doses and found they did lose some muscle overall, but they lost proportionally more fat than muscle, which is actually a good sign for body composition. The researchers still point to nutrition and exercise as the best ways to protect muscle along the way.  (4)

The takeaway is simple: do not panic about muscle loss GLP-1 headlines, but do give your body the tools to stay strong.

Habit 2: Keep Protein Consistent

Protein intake on GLP-1 can take some planning because your meals may feel different than they used to.

Some days, a full meal may feel easy. Other days, smaller meals or snacks may feel more realistic. That is where protein anchors can help.

A protein anchor is a food or meal you can repeat often. Examples include:

  • Greek yogurt or cottage cheese at breakfast
  • Eggs, tofu, or beans in a quick lunch
  • Chicken, fish, turkey, lentils, or tempeh at dinner
  • A smoothie with protein if solid food feels hard
  • Edamame, tuna, hummus, or cheese as a snack

Getting enough protein and doing some strength training work together; one helps your body make better use of the other to build and maintain muscle. (5)

“It’s also important to note that not everyone needs the same protein target. Your needs can vary based on body size, activity level, age, and training goals,” Basbaum says. (5) 

A practical Winter Arc goal could be: “I will include protein at two meals most days.”

That is specific. It is flexible. And it is much more useful than trying to overhaul every meal at once.

Habit 3: Keep Movement Easy To Repeat

Strength training can be your anchor, but it does not have to be your only movement. Walking, cycling, dancing, swimming, yoga, or indoor treadmill time can all be part of an exercise and GLP-1 routine. 

A useful target: about 150 minutes of moderate movement a week, plus two strength sessions.  But let’s be honest, that can sound like a lot, especially in winter. So make it smaller. (3)

Try:

  • A 10-minute walk after lunch
  • A short indoor walk while listening to a podcast
  • One strength session during the week and one on the weekend
  • A stretch routine on side-effect days
  • A quick walk before the sun goes down

Your GLP-1 exercise program does not need to be perfect. It just needs to be repeatable.

Habit 4: Track What Helps, Not Everything Perfectly

Tracking can be a helpful tool on a GLP-1 journey, but it should not become another extreme rule.

The point is not to prove you had a perfect day. The point is to notice patterns.

For example, tracking can help you see:

  • Whether you are getting protein regularly
  • Which meals feel best before or after workouts
  • Whether symptoms line up with certain foods or medication timing
  • How often you are strength training
  • Where your routine tends to slip during busy weeks

Try prompts like:

  • “How am I doing on protein today?”
  • “What is an easy dinner if I am not very hungry?”
  • “What can I eat before a light strength workout?”
  • “What is one simple habit I can focus on this week?”

That is the better version of “lock in”: use your own patterns to make the next choice easier.

A Simple 4-Week GLP-1 Winter Arc Plan

You do not need to plan every day from October through December. Start with four weeks.

Week 1: Choose your three habits

Pick one habit for movement, one for protein, and one for tracking.

For example:

  • Movement: two short strength sessions
  • Protein: protein at breakfast
  • Tracking: log meals and GLP-1 dose on dose day

Keep it simple enough that you can do it during a busy week.

Week 2: Make protein easier

Choose two or three protein anchors you can repeat.

This could be Greek yogurt, eggs, a ready-to-drink protein shake, tofu, rotisserie chicken, tuna packets, lentils, or cottage cheese.

The goal is not to eat the same thing every day forever. It is to lower the number of decisions you have to make.

Week 3: Build your strength rhythm

This week, focus on making your strength routine feel familiar. Keep the moves simple, repeat the basics, and notice what feels doable.

If your energy is lower, use the lighter version. If you feel good, stick with the same routine and finish with confidence. 

“Exercise and GLP-1 habits do not need to be extreme to be useful. Consistency is the win here,” Basbaum notes.

Week 4: Holiday-proof the plan

This is where your Winter Arc becomes realistic.

Make a backup plan for cold weather, travel, parties, or low-energy days. Save a few easy high-protein meals. Keep resistance bands nearby. Pick an indoor walking option. Decide what “good enough” looks like during a holiday week.

A good plan should bend. If it breaks every time life happens, it is too rigid.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

  • Is the Winter Arc a good idea if you are on a GLP-1?

It can be, as long as you use it as a flexible habit-building period instead of an extreme challenge. The Winter Arc is generally framed as an October-through-December period of focus before the new year, but GLP-1 users may need to adjust routines around appetite, digestion, energy, side effects, and their prescriber’s guidance. (1, 2, 6)

  • How can you help prevent muscle loss on GLP-1?

You may not be able to prevent every change in muscle during weight loss. But you can support it by strength training, eating enough protein, and avoiding overly restrictive eating. Research on GLP-1 medications backs up pairing treatment with good nutrition and regular movement. (3,  4,  5)

  • What is a good GLP-1 exercise program for beginners?

A beginner-friendly GLP-1 exercise program might include two days of full-body strength training and a few short walks each week — roughly two strength sessions plus about 150 minutes of moderate movement is a solid target.  (3)

  • How should you approach protein intake on GLP-1?

Protein intake on GLP-1 should be realistic and personalized. A good starting habit is to include protein at meals or snacks throughout the day, especially if your usual intake feels lower than before. Protein and strength training work together — getting enough of one helps your body make better use of the other.  (5)

  • How can you stay consistent during the Winter Arc?

Staying consistent is easier when you track the habits that help you make better choices. That might mean logging meals, protein, movement, symptoms, or medication timing.

MyFitnessPal’s GLP-1 Support can help you track GLP-1 medication details and side effects, while AI Nutrition Coach can help with simple nutrition questions based on your logged meals.

Bottom Line

The Winter Arc does not need to be intense to be useful.

For GLP-1 users, the best version is not about restriction. It is about choosing a few habits that help you feel supported: strength training, protein consistency, realistic movement, and tracking that helps you notice patterns.

Lock in gently. Keep the habits small enough to repeat. Let muscle support be one goal, not the whole story.

Start With One Big Visual Moment

Anchor your board with a single ‘showstopper’—a visual centerpiece that sets the eerie mood immediately. Instead of scattering themes randomly, let this one element dictate your color palette and vibe. 

For example, place a serving of Pumpkin Soup in pumpkin-shaped mugs as your pumpkin patch centerpiece, or set out a mini cauldron filled with Protein Pickle Dip or Cottage Cheese Queso. Alternatively, build a sweet crypt anchored by Cottage Cheese Cookie Dough, or feature a dramatic pitcher of Ginger Citrus Mocktail as your witches’ brew. 

Once your anchor is set, it serves as the gravitational pull for the rest of your snacks, making it easy to arrange extras like tombstone bites or eyeball eggs around it.

Choose Two or Three Recipes, Max

You do not need seven different homemade dishes to impress; two or three are plenty to anchor a balanced spread. To build a cohesive board with zero overwhelm, pick one item from each of these three categories:

  • The Savory Anchor: Start with a hearty, dippable base like the Protein Pickle Dip or Cottage Cheese Queso.
  • The Substantial Bite: Choose one warm, protein-packed main, such as Air-Fryer Boneless Buffalo Wings or Chickpea Salad Sandwich bites for a plant-forward option.
  • The Sweet Finish: Balance the salt with something creamy like Cottage Cheese Cookie Dough or a refreshing Ginger Citrus Mocktail.

“A grazing table should still feel like enough food, not just a collection of bites. Adding a few protein-rich options, like deviled eggs, wings, yogurt-based dips, cheese, or chickpea bites, can help the spread feel more satisfying,” Emily Sullivan, RD, MyFitnessPal Registered Dietitian, says. (3) 

By sticking to this ‘one savory, one hearty, one sweet’ formula, you ensure a variety of textures and flavors while keeping your prep time manageable. Need more inspiration? You can browse endless options via MyFitnessPal Recipe Discovery.

Make It Feel Grown-Up

Halloween food gets cartoonish fast. Ground it with marinated olives, sharp cheese, spicy pickles, herbed nuts, whole-grain crackers, roasted red peppers, a fresh-herb dip, or a mocktail in a coupe glass.

Then add just one or two playful touches: olive spiders, candy eyes, or skeleton hands by the tongs.

Keep the food recognizable. Just give it a costume.

How To Arrange It

Start with anything in cups or bowls. That means the pumpkin soup cups, dips, olives, pickles, nuts, and sweets. These pieces give the board height and shape.

If you are serving soup in pumpkin-shaped mugs, set them on a tray or platter first so they are easier to move and refill.

Then place the warm or prepped foods, each in its own spot. Fill the gaps with crackers, apple slices, grapes, carrots, cucumbers, celery, pepper strips, berries, and mini chocolate.

Add the Halloween details last because cracker tombstones can go soggy fast. Keep candy in one corner so it looks intentional.

Keep It Safe

Refresh the board instead of letting it sit untouched for hours.

You do not need every item on the board to be themed. One strong visual moment, like pumpkin soup cups or a cauldron dip, can make the whole table feel festive.

“Cold foods should stay at 40°F or colder, hot foods should stay at 140°F or warmer, and perishable foods should be tossed if they have been out for more than 2 hours unless they are kept hot or cold,” Sullivan says (2).

That covers dips, cheese, wings, and anything egg- or dairy-based.

Easy fix: put out smaller portions, keep backups in the fridge, and refresh the board halfway through the party.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

  • What are good Halloween appetizers for adults?

Savory dips, mini sandwiches, wings, skewers, stuffed peppers, cheese, olives, spiced nuts, vegetables, crackers, and a sweet or two all work well. Aim for foods that are easy to eat one-handed while mingling. For more variety, include a mix of protein foods, dairy, vegetables, fruit, healthy fats such as nuts, seeds and olives,, and whole grains. (1)

  • How do I make a board look spooky?

Use dark trays, black bowls, cracker tombstones, olive spiders, candy eyes, skeleton hands, or a spooky label. You do not need every food to be themed. A few details can carry the whole board.

  • How far ahead can I prep?

Wash produce, make dips, mix the mocktail base, and cut bread or crackers ahead. Add sparkling water last, and wait to assemble anything crunchy until closer to serving.

  • How long can it sit out?

Perishable foods can sit out for up to 2 hours unless they are kept hot or cold. If the temperature is above 90°F, that window drops to 1 hour. (2)

Skip the standard snack-board formula.

Is isometric calf strength testing an effective predictor of athletic performance?

(Image: Instagram)

S&C expert Professor Stephen Patterson recently shared an interesting LinkedIn post highlighting new research that questions the effectiveness of isometric calf strength testing as a predictor of athletic performance.

The research found that isometric calf strength explained approximately 9% of drop jump performance and just 2% of jump height and sprint speed, with none of these links reaching statistical significance. Patterson highlighted a key limitation of conventional isometric calf strength testing: the lack of specificity to the mechanical demands of sprinting and jumping. Many isometric calf strength assessments are performed with the ankle positioned close to neutral, at approximately 2.5° of dorsiflexion. In contrast, during sprinting and jumping, peak ankle torque occurs at approximately 16–21° of dorsiflexion, under substantially higher movement velocities and within the stretch-shortening cycle. Consequently, these conventional isometric assessments may fail to adequately replicate the joint angles, contraction velocities, and neuromuscular demands associated with explosive athletic movements.

Based on these findings, Patterson suggested that if the primary purpose of isometric calf strength testing is to predict sprinting or jumping performance, he would stop using it. Importantly, this does not necessarily invalidate isometric calf strength testing as a measure of calf strength but rather questions its usefulness as a standalone predictor of dynamic athletic performance.

Is There Healthy Food at McDonald’s?

Yes, you can find healthier options at McDonald’s. It’s possible by choosing lower-calorie items and smaller portions. (1)

If you crave those iconic fries or a classic burger, it’s okay to indulge once in a while. Making smart choices and simple swaps at fast food joints like McDonald’s means you can honor your cravings in a way that feels good for your mind and body. (2, 3, 4)

Traditionally made with rice, congee is a comfort food staple in Chinese cuisine, but this lighter take on the classic porridge swaps rice for oats. With the addition of spices, veggies and lean protein with chicken, you have a delicious meal in 15 minutes for about 400 calories.

Active time: 15 minutes | Total time: 15 minutes

Chicken Congee

Ingredients

  • 2 tbsp olive oil
  • 3 cloves garlic, chopped fine
  • 2 shallots, chopped fine
  • 3.5oz (100g) boneless, skinless cooked chicken breast, minced 
  • 1/2 tsp low-sodium soy sauce 
  • 2 cups (500g) water
  • 1/2 tsp low-sodium chicken bouillon powder
  • 1 cup (100g) quick-cooking oats
  • 1 tsp sesame oil
  • 3 celery stalks, thinly sliced on the diagonal
  • Ground black pepper, to taste

Directions

Heat the olive oil in a pan and add the chopped garlic and shallot to the pan. Sauté until both are browned. Add the minced chicken and soy sauce. Mix well and set aside.

Cook the oats with the water and chicken bouillon powder until thickened to your liking. Add the seasoned chicken to the porridge and stir well. 

Divide into 2 bowls and garnish with the celery, sesame oil and black pepper to taste. Serve hot.

Serves: 2 | Serving Size: 1/2 of recipe

Can a one-bowl meal really satisfy? This one will. It starts with a base of nutty farro and tender sweet potato, topped with chicken breast and finished with a tangy, herbal chimichurri sauce.

Farro is a great grain option because it contains a good amount of protein and fiber (1). You can also add any roasted vegetable — like broccoli or squash — to this meal to up the fiber content.

Active time: 45 minutes | Total time: 1 hour

Chicken and Sweet Potato Farro Bowl

Ingredients

  • 1 cup (208g) semi-pearled farro
  • 1 medium (130g) sweet potato, cubed (and peeled if desired)
  • 1 lb (450g) boneless, skinless chicken breast
  • 1 tbsp olive oil, divided
  • 2 tsp chili powder
  • 1 1/2 cups (36g) fresh parsley, lightly packed
  • 1 clove garlic, chopped
  • 1/4 tsp crushed red pepper flakes
  • 3 tbsp red wine vinegar

Directions

Preheat the oven to 400ºF (200ºC).

Put 3 cups of water in a medium saucepan and bring to a boil. Add the farro and sweet potato cubes and return to a boil, then cover and reduce the heat to low to simmer for 20 minutes. The farro should be tender. Drain in a wire mesh strainer and let cool. The mixture can be stored in the refrigerator, tightly covered, for up to a week.

Line a sheet pan with parchment, then place the chicken on the pan. Drizzle with 1 tsp olive oil and massage to coat, then sprinkle with the chili powder and rub to coat. Bake for 15–20 minutes, until the chicken is cooked through and an instant-read thermometer reads 165°F (74°C) when inserted in the thickest part of the breast. Cool on a rack until safe to handle. Shred into pieces and reserve.

For the chimichurri, place the parsley and garlic in the bowl of a food processor. Process to mince finely, scraping down the sides to make a fine paste. Add the crushed red pepper, red wine vinegar and remaining 2 tsp of olive oil. Process until smooth. (Makes about 1/2 cup.) 

Serves: 4 | Serving Size: 1 cup farro and sweet potato mixture, plus 1 cup chicken and 2 tbsp chimichurri sauce
